## Summary
This is a subject-to purchase of a fourplex at $310,000, taking over a $205,000 loan at 3.2% and paying the seller $15,000 in cash plus a seller note. On the stated figures the deal covers its debt comfortably, but it depends on the existing loan staying in place and on a balloon being paid in month 84.

## Returns
Stabilized net operating income is $30,680, a 9.9% cap rate on the purchase price and a 9.8% yield on total cost. Stabilized cash flow is $11,295 a year, or $941 a month. Cash needed at closing is $18,100, which is also the most cash in the deal at once. That gives a cash on cash return of 62.4% and a payback of 19 months. Over the 120-month hold, total profit is $320,530, with an IRR of 90.4%, an equity multiple of 4.37x and an annualized return on cash of 34%. The exit assumes a sale at $416,614, with net proceeds of $245,925, built on 3% annual rent growth and 3% annual appreciation. These returns are very high because so little cash goes in. Treat them as a sign of thin equity, not a measure of safety.

## Financing
The existing loan of $205,000 at 3.2% stays in the seller's name. The seller note is at 4.5% and carries a balloon. Debt service coverage is 1.79, and break even occupancy is 65.6%, so the property can absorb a good deal of vacancy while both loans are paid.

## Risks
1. **Due-on-sale exposure.** The seller's lender can call the existing loan due when the deed transfers. If that happens, the $205,000 would need to be paid or refinanced, likely at a much higher rate than 3.2%. With $18,100 in the deal, there is little room to cure that. The coverage ratio of 1.79 assumes the low rate stays.
2. **Balloon in month 84.** A $78,324 balloon payment comes due, well before the 120-month hold ends. The plan must include a refinance or sale by then. The balloon falls due before the exit the return figures assume.
3. **Growth assumptions.** The exit price and the returns depend on 3% rent growth and 3% appreciation holding for the full hold. A flat market would reduce profit and weaken the ability to refinance the balloon.
4. **Seller dependence.** The seller's loan stays in the seller's name, so the seller's missed payments or credit problems become your problem.

## Bottom line
The operating numbers are sound, with good coverage and a low break even. The structure is the weak point: a call on the existing loan or a failed refinance at month 84 could force a sale. Proceed only with a funded plan for both events, and not on the strength of the 90.4% IRR alone.
