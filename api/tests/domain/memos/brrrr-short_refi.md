## Summary
This is a BRRRR on a ranch at 2210 Kohler Ave, Sheboygan, WI: a $112,000 purchase, a $46,000 rehab over 4 months, and an after repair value of $165,000. The operating numbers look sound, but the refinance does not return the investor's capital, so this works as a long hold, not a recycle-the-cash deal.

## Returns
- Total profit over the hold is $86,333, with an IRR of 13% and an equity multiple of 2.76x.
- Annualized return on cash is 10.7%.
- Stabilized net operating income is $13,151, a cap rate of 11.7% on the purchase price and a yield on total cost of 8.2%.
- Stabilized cash flow is $2,210 a year, or $184 a month. Cash on cash return is 4.5%.
- Payback is 120 months, the full hold period. Most of the profit depends on the exit: a sale price of $219,572 and net proceeds of $98,476, which assume 3% yearly appreciation.

## Financing
- The purchase loan is 85% of cost at 11%. Cash needed at closing is $22,064.
- The refinance comes after 6 months at 75% of value and a 7% rate.
- The most cash in the deal at once is $48,939. Cash left in the deal after the refinance is also $48,939, so none of it comes back.
- The debt service coverage ratio is 1.32 on the refinanced loan.

## Risks
1. **Refinance shortfall.** The new loan falls $19,326 short of paying off the first loan, and the investor must cover that. The refinance is sized on the appraised value. If the appraisal comes in below $165,000, the gap grows.
2. **Capital stays locked up.** With the full $48,939 still in the deal, the investor cannot redeploy it into the next purchase. This defeats the main purpose of the strategy.
3. **Thin cash flow.** At $184 a month, one major repair or a few months of vacancy would erase a year of surplus.
4. **Coverage margin.** Break even occupancy is 82.4%, against a 5% vacancy allowance. That leaves a real but limited cushion.
5. **Expensive bridge debt.** The 11% purchase loan runs through the rehab and the wait for the refinance. Any rehab overrun or delay adds to a cost the table does not capture.
6. **Exit dependence.** Much of the 13% IRR comes from the sale at the end of 120 months, not from yearly cash flow.

## Bottom line
The property produces income and covers its debt, but it fails the BRRRR test of pulling the cash back out. I would proceed only if the investor is content to leave $48,939 in a modest 4.5% cash-on-cash hold, with the return resting on appreciation and a sale ten years out. Otherwise, renegotiate the price or trim the rehab.
